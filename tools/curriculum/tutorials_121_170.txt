121|prefix + verb: meaning, stress and separability belong together|Prefixes can suggest entry, exit, removal or change, but do not determine every word's meaning mechanically. Learn a prefixed verb as a lexical unit, including whether its prefix separates.~Stress can distinguish different verbs with the same spelling. Separable umfahren, fährt … um, means knock over by driving into something; inseparable umfahren, umfährt, means drive around. Separable übersetzen can mean ferry across, while inseparable übersetzen means translate.|Der Bus umfährt die Baustelle. => The bus drives around the roadworks.~Der Fahrer fährt den Pfosten um. => The driver knocks the post over.|A prefix's spatial meaning is a clue, not a guarantee; check the particular verb and its sentence pattern.
122|stem + noun suffix → noun meaning and likely gender|Suffixes can help identify nouns: -heit, -keit, -schaft and -ung are normally feminine. -er often names a person or agent; a corresponding female person form can use -erin.~Do not turn these tendencies into a universal gender algorithm. -nis includes das Erlebnis and die Erlaubnis; -tum includes das Eigentum and der Reichtum. Learn the article and plural with each word. Recognition of families helps infer meaning without inventing unattested words.|Aus sicher wird die Sicherheit. => The adjective secure gives the noun security.~Das Erlebnis bleibt in Erinnerung. => The experience remains in memory.|A familiar suffix can suggest gender, but an individual noun's lexical information overrides a guess.
123|word family + adjective suffix/prefix|Adjective formation gives useful meaning clues: -los and -frei often mark absence; -reich and -voll indicate abundance or a quality; -bar often marks a possible action.~Other suffixes include -ig, -lich, -isch, -haft and -sam. Their exact meanings are lexical: hilfreich means helpful, mühsam laborious. Un- often negates a quality, but not every imaginable un-word is established. An adjective before a noun still needs its normal grammatical ending.|Die Tasche ist waschbar. => The bag can be washed.~Der Text ist fehlerfrei. => The text has no errors.|A suffix helps you interpret a known word family; it does not license forming every possible combination.
124|-weise / -maßen / -wärts / -lang / -s|Derived adverbs can express manner or extent, direction, duration and recurring time. Examples are normalerweise, gewissermaßen, rückwärts, stundenlang and morgens.~As adverbs, these forms do not take adjective endings to agree with a noun. Contrast an attributive adjective such as eine lange Fahrt with the duration adverb stundenlang. Learn the whole word's meaning; a suffix is a useful clue rather than a complete translation rule.|Normalerweise arbeitet sie vormittags. => She normally works in the mornings.~Wir haben stundenlang gewartet. => We waited for hours.|Do not confuse stundenlang, a duration, with dreimal, an occurrence count, or täglich, a recurring schedule.
125|abbreviation/shortened word + textual context|Notices and advertisements compress common words: z. B. means zum Beispiel, d. h. das heißt, ca. circa and inkl. inklusive. Learn frequent transport and address forms such as Pkw, Lkw, PLZ and Nr.~Some forms use periods while initialisms such as EU do not; clippings such as Info or Uni behave like ordinary words. Context can disambiguate an abbreviation with several meanings. The target is understanding common notices, not memorizing an unlimited list.|Der Preis gilt inkl. Frühstück. => The price includes breakfast.~Bitte geben Sie Ihre PLZ an. => Please enter your postal code.|Do not guess an unfamiliar abbreviation only from its letters; use the surrounding topic and sentence.
126|noun-like use → capital; formal Sie/Ihnen/Ihr → capital|Capitalize nominalized infinitives and adjectives: das Lesen, beim Ausfüllen, etwas Neues, die Angestellte. A verb or adjective in its ordinary use remains lowercase inside the sentence.~Formal address uses Sie, Ihnen and the possessive Ihr with a capital letter, including inflected Ihre/Ihrem. Third-person sie, ihnen and ihre normally stay lowercase inside a sentence. Sentence-initial capitalization alone does not prove that a pronoun is formal address.|Vielen Dank für Ihre Hilfe beim Ausfüllen. => Thank you for your help with filling it in. (formal)~Ich habe etwas Neues gelernt. => I learned something new.|Compare die angestellte Frau with die Angestellte; grammatical function, not the word's origin, decides nominal capitalization.
127|comma marks finite and clause-like infinitive boundaries|Separate subordinate and relative clauses with commas; an inserted clause needs both an opening and a closing comma. Keep those boundaries when clauses meet coordinating words such as und.~Under the current spelling model, clause-like expanded zu-infinitive groups also take commas. Groups introduced by um, ohne or statt are clear examples. Distinguish a separate infinitive clause from part of a multi-verb predicate, as in scheinen or nicht brauchen, where no such comma is inserted.|Das Buch, das ich lese, ist spannend. => The book I am reading is exciting.~Ich spare, um ein Fahrrad zu kaufen. => I am saving to buy a bicycle.|Set commas by grammatical structure, not wherever you would happen to pause when speaking.
128|noun compound: together; digit/letter combination: linking hyphens|Ordinary noun compounds are written together: Haustürschlüssel, Bewerbungsgespräch. When a digit or letter forms part of a multi-part compound, connect the parts with hyphens: 5-Euro-Schein, E-Mail-Adresse.~A suspended compound part keeps an Ergänzungsstrich: Hin- und Rückfahrt. This indicates the shared omitted element, not a random decorative dash. Some complex words permit additional clarifying hyphens, so do not assume every hyphenated variant is wrong. Learn established word divisions for verb combinations separately.|Wir suchen eine Zwei-Zimmer-Wohnung. => We are looking for a two-room apartment.~Sie bucht die Hin- und Rückfahrt. => She books the outward and return journey.|Spaces alone do not normally join a German compound noun; keep writing rules separate from pronunciation pauses.
129|present event passive: wird/werden + past participle|The event passive presents an action or process with the affected thing as subject. Conjugate werden for that subject; the participle describes what is being done.~An agent may be added with von plus dative, but need not be named. Distinguish Futur I, which uses werden plus an infinitive, and a resulting state with sein plus participle. Recognition of process versus future is the main target, not producing elaborate passive sentences.|Die Unterlagen werden geprüft. => The documents are being checked.~Der Brief wird von Anna geschrieben. => The letter is being written by Anna.|Check the final form: geschrieben is a participle, while schreiben is an infinitive.
130|past event passive: wurde/wurden + past participle|Präteritum event passive uses the past forms of werden: wurde, wurdest, wurde, wurden, wurdet, wurden. The participle itself does not change with the subject.~Use the subject's number to choose wurde or wurden in third person. The construction describes a past process or event, not merely an existing past state. War geschlossen describes a state; wurde geschlossen describes the closing event. Read reports for this distinction before prioritizing production.|Die Straße wurde gestern gesperrt. => The road was closed to traffic yesterday.~Die Fenster wurden geöffnet. => The windows were opened.|Wurde is the passive auxiliary's past form; geworden is not a substitute for it in this construction.
131|perfect event passive: ist/sind + participle + worden|The perfect event passive uses a form of sein, a lexical participle and worden. The subject is still the affected person or thing; choose ist/sind by its number.~Worden is the special participial form used for passive werden. Lexical werden meaning become uses geworden instead. Without worden, sein plus a participle can describe a state or form an active perfect of a sein-verb. Interpret the whole group rather than one isolated word.|Der Termin ist verschoben worden. => The appointment has been rescheduled.~Sie ist Ärztin geworden. => She has become a doctor.|Do not write ist geprüft geworden when you mean has been checked; use ist geprüft worden.
132|finite modal + … + past participle + werden|A modal passive expresses necessity, permission or possibility for an action affecting the subject. The finite modal agrees with that subject; the group closes with participle plus infinitive werden.~Interpret müssen, dürfen and können separately. Nicht müssen removes necessity, while nicht dürfen prohibits the action. An impersonal passive may have no named participant: Hier darf nicht geparkt werden. Recognizing the requirement or permission matters more than fluent production of long verb groups.|Der Antrag muss geprüft werden. => The application must be checked.~Die Datei darf nicht gelöscht werden. => The file must not be deleted.|Werden remains an infinitive after the modal; do not replace it with the perfect-passive word worden.
133|state: sein + participle; event: werden + participle|State passive describes a condition resulting from an action: die Tür ist geschlossen. Event passive presents the action of producing that condition: die Tür wird geschlossen.~Use context and the lexical verb to distinguish state passive from active Perfekt. Sie ist angekommen is an active arrival, not a passive condition created by an agent. A state does not assert when or by whom the earlier action happened. Recognition of these readings is the priority.|Das Zimmer ist aufgeräumt. => The room is tidy, as the result of tidying.~Das Zimmer wird aufgeräumt. => The room is being tidied.|Do not classify every sein plus participle as passive; some verbs form their active perfect with sein.
134|infinitive + -d + adjective ending|Partizip I generally derives from the infinitive plus -d: warten → wartend. Before a noun, add the required adjective ending: die wartenden Gäste.~It describes an ongoing action or active property associated with the noun, usually simultaneous with the relevant reference event. It is not itself a finite verb and does not create a separate present-tense clause. Decode the participial stem and the inflection separately. Understanding the attribute is more important than producing dense phrases.|Das schlafende Kind liegt im Bett. => The sleeping child is lying in bed.~Die steigenden Preise machen uns Sorgen. => The rising prices worry us.|Do not read a present participle as proof that an action has already finished.
135|past participle + adjective ending before a noun|A Partizip II can describe a noun through a completed event or resulting condition: das reparierte Gerät. It takes ordinary adjective endings according to its determiner and case.~Transitive participles often have a passive/result meaning: the device was repaired. Intransitive change verbs can have an active completed meaning: die angekommenen Gäste are guests who arrived. The participle alone is an attribute, not a finite perfect tense; look for the sentence's actual finite verb.|Die beschädigten Geräte werden ersetzt. => The damaged devices are being replaced.~Die angekommenen Gäste warten draußen. => The guests who have arrived are waiting outside.|Not every past-participial attribute is passive; identify the lexical verb and the noun's role.
136|expanded participial phrase + head noun|In a compressed attribute, time, place and agent information can appear before the participle and head noun. First locate the final head noun, then find its participial modifier.~Attach the other expressions to the event they describe and expand mentally into a relative clause. The head noun's case comes from the larger sentence; an internal preposition may govern another noun independently. Recognition and unpacking are the priorities, not writing increasingly long compressed phrases.|Die gestern im Zug gefundenen Unterlagen liegen hier. => The documents found on the train yesterday are here.~Das vom Techniker reparierte Gerät funktioniert. => The device repaired by the technician works.|A noun inside the attribute, such as Zug or Techniker, is not automatically the phrase's head.
137|reported present: sei / habe / komme …|Konjunktiv I can mark another speaker's claim in formal reporting. Common third-person singular forms are sei for sein, habe for haben and an -e form such as komme or arbeite.~The reporter attributes the statement without thereby guaranteeing its truth; the form does not automatically mean the claim is false. Distinguish indicative statements and hypothetical Konjunktiv II wishes. Some Konjunktiv I forms coincide with indicative forms, so context remains important. Recognition is the target.|Die Zeitung berichtet, der Fahrer sei unverletzt. => The newspaper reports that the driver is uninjured.~Anna sagt, sie habe Zeit. => Anna says that she has time.|Sei in a report belongs to Konjunktiv I; wäre is a different, hypothetical form.
138|reported past: habe/sei + past participle|A reported completed event can use the Konjunktiv I auxiliary habe or sei plus a participle. Keep the lexical verb's usual haben/sein choice.~Person and reference must still match the original speaker: quoted ich may become er or sie. The construction marks attributed past information, not necessarily a past event the reporter personally witnessed. Contrast the reporter's own indicative hat/ist and an unreal-past hätte/wäre. Practice recognizing whose statement is being relayed.|Der Zeuge sagt, er habe den Mann gesehen. => The witness says that he saw the man.~Sie erklärt, sie sei früh angekommen. => She explains that she arrived early.|Do not select habe for every reported past event; movement/change verbs can require sei.
139|reported future: werde + infinitive|In formal indirect speech, third-person singular werde plus infinitive attributes a future plan or prediction to another speaker. The lexical infinitive describes the future event.~Distinguish indicative wird plus infinitive, passive wird plus participle and reported past habe/sei plus participle. A reporting verb such as erklären or berichten helps establish the source. The form attributes a claim; it does not certify that the future event will occur. Recognition remains the learning priority.|Sie erklärt, sie werde später zurückrufen. => She explains that she will call back later.~Die Firma teilt mit, sie werde neue Stellen schaffen. => The company announces that it will create new jobs.|Compare werde prüfen with werde geprüft: the final form changes the construction you must interpret.
140|unreal past: hätte/wäre + past participle|Konjunktiv II past imagines a different past or expresses regret. Use hätte or wäre according to the lexical verb's normal perfect auxiliary, plus its participle.~A wenn-condition and its result can both use these forms. Distinguish the indicative earlier-past hatte/war from hypothetical hätte/wäre. A wish can stand alone with doch. In these exercises, understand the imagined alternative rather than assuming the sentence reports that alternative as fact.|Wenn ich früher gefahren wäre, hätte ich den Zug erreicht. => If I had left earlier, I would have caught the train.~Hätte ich doch angerufen! => If only I had called!|The participle makes this a past hypothetical, unlike present-state ich hätte Zeit.
141|hat/hätte + lexical infinitive + modal infinitive|When a modal governs another verb in a perfect group, it commonly uses a replacement infinitive rather than its ordinary participle: hat arbeiten müssen.~Konjunktiv II can add an unreal-past meaning: hätte anrufen sollen. In subordinate clauses with these verb clusters, the auxiliary can come before the two infinitives: weil er hat arbeiten müssen. Identify the lexical action and modal meaning separately. This unusual order is a recognition target, not a reason to apply it to every subordinate clause.|Ich habe länger warten müssen. => I had to wait longer.~Sie hätte früher anrufen sollen. => She should have called earlier.|Do not mechanically replace müssen with gemusst when it governs another infinitive in this past modal group.
142|werden + participle + haben/sein = Futur II|Futur II can view an event as completed by a future reference point. Conjugate werden, then use the lexical participle and its infinitive auxiliary haben or sein.~The same construction can express an inference about an already completed event, often with wohl and a present evidential context. A deadline favors future completion; an explanation of present evidence favors inference. Distinguish this from ordinary future werden plus a bare lexical infinitive. Recognition is sufficient here.|Bis Freitag wird sie alles erledigt haben. => By Friday she will have finished everything.~Er ist nicht da; er wird schon gegangen sein. => He is not here; he has probably already left.|The final haben/sein belongs to the completed-event perspective; it is not a second finite verb.
143|sein zu: can/must be done; haben zu: must do|Sein plus a zu-infinitive often expresses passive possibility or necessity. Words such as leicht, problemlos or unbedingt and the surrounding instructions help decide which reading is intended.~Haben plus a zu-infinitive places an obligation on its subject. Contrast these modal-like predicates with ordinary perfect haben plus participle and noun complements such as Zeit haben, etwas zu tun. Do not assume every zu after haben/sein carries the same meaning. Interpret the whole frame before translating.|Die Aufgabe ist leicht zu lösen. => The task can easily be solved.~Du hast die Frist einzuhalten. => You are required to meet the deadline.|Die Regeln sind zu beachten usually states a requirement; context distinguishes necessity from mere feasibility.
144|noun + support verb = one lexical meaning|Expressions such as eine Entscheidung treffen, eine Frage stellen and Rücksicht nehmen function as conventional units. Their support verb may carry less independent meaning than a literal translation suggests.~Learn the noun, verb, required article and preposition together. Contrast availability, zur Verfügung stehen, with making something available, zur Verfügung stellen. The finite verb still follows ordinary tense/person rules. Connect each phrase to a simpler meaning where possible, such as entscheiden for eine Entscheidung treffen.|Wir müssen eine Entscheidung treffen. => We must make a decision.~Mehrere Termine stehen zur Verfügung. => Several appointments are available.|Do not freely swap geben, machen and nehmen simply because English uses one general verb for several expressions.
145|sodass / so + adjective/adverb + dass → consequence|Sodass links a result to the preceding event. So … dass connects the degree of a quality with its consequence: so laut, dass …~Both constructions introduce a subordinate clause with final finite verb order and a comma. A consequence is presented as resulting from the situation; damit and um … zu instead express an intended purpose. The spelling so dass is also possible in appropriate uses, so focus on the relation and clause structure rather than declaring every separated form wrong.|Es war so laut, dass ich nichts hörte. => It was so loud that I heard nothing.~Der Bus fiel aus, sodass wir laufen mussten. => The bus was canceled, so we had to walk.|An intended result need not be achieved; a consequence clause presents the result as part of what happened.
146|main result, indem + method clause|Indem tells how or by what means the main action is achieved. Its finite verb goes last and the clause is separated by a comma.~The method is not just another event happening at the same time. Contrast temporal während for overlap, weil for a reason and damit for a goal. The method clause can have its own explicit subject, although man or a repeated actor is common. Recognize the means–result relation rather than merely matching words.|Man spart Energie, indem man weniger heizt. => You save energy by heating less.~Sie lernt Wörter, indem sie eigene Sätze schreibt. => She learns words by writing her own sentences.|Ask how the result is achieved; an answer that only says when something happens is not the same relation.
147|je + comparative … verb, desto/umso + comparative … verb …|Je introduces a subordinate comparative clause with its finite verb last. Desto or umso introduces the correlated comparative phrase in the main clause; the finite verb follows that whole phrase.~The construction links two changes. They can move in the same or opposite directions: more practice can mean greater confidence, while earlier booking can mean a lower price. Read both comparative meanings before inferring the relation. Recognition of the correlation and word order is the priority.|Je früher wir buchen, desto günstiger wird es. => The earlier we book, the cheaper it becomes.~Je mehr ich übe, umso sicherer werde ich. => The more I practice, the more confident I become.|Desto does not simply mean both quantities increase; one comparative may describe a decrease.
148|als ob … Konjunktiv II; shortened als + finite verb …|An unreal comparison describes an appearance, not an established fact: er tut so, als ob … Konjunktiv II forms such as wäre, hätte or wüsste are common markers.~After als ob, the finite verb comes last. In the shortened pattern with als alone, the verb comes immediately after als: als wüsste er alles. The described appearance may or may not be true; the sentence does not prove it false either. Practice interpretation rather than elaborate production.|Er tut so, als ob er alles wüsste. => He acts as if he knew everything.~Sie spricht, als wäre sie die Leiterin. => She speaks as if she were the manager.|Do not give shortened als the same verb-final order as the full als ob-clause.
149|während: temporal while or contrasting whereas|Während can contrast two general facts, preferences or situations instead of primarily marking simultaneous events. The finite verb still goes last in either use.~Use context to identify the intended relation: general opposite preferences favor whereas; a specific shared moment favors while. Both readings can sometimes coexist, so the conjunction alone does not mechanically determine one exclusive meaning. An adversative clause can often be paraphrased with dagegen or aber.|Während ich lieber lese, hört sie lieber Musik. => Whereas I prefer reading, she prefers listening to music.~Während ich gerade lese, klingelt das Telefon. => While I am reading, the telephone rings.|Do not classify every während-clause as a time expression just because its form matches the temporal construction.
150|main event, ohne dass + subject + … + finite verb|Ohne dass says that an accompanying circumstance does not occur. Unlike a shared-subject infinitive group, it can explicitly name a different actor.~Keep the final finite verb and the comma boundary. Compare ohne … zu when the understood actor is shared. The main event occurs without the subordinate fact; the clause does not express its purpose or necessarily its cause. In this unit, focus on interpreting which event is absent and who would have performed it.|Er ging, ohne dass ich es bemerkte. => He left without my noticing it.~Sie bezahlte, ohne dass jemand sie darum bat. => She paid without anyone asking her to.|Identify the two subjects; do not automatically assign the main subject to the action after ohne dass.
151|main course, statt dass + own subject + final finite verb|Statt dass names an unused or unwanted alternative with a full finite clause. The main clause gives the proposed or actual course that replaces it.~It is especially useful when the two actions need different explicit subjects. With a shared understood actor, statt … zu is often possible instead. Use a comma and final verb order. A recommendation in the main clause does not automatically assert that the preferred action has already happened.|Wir sollten helfen, statt dass sie alles allein macht. => We should help instead of her doing everything alone.~Statt dass alle reden, sollte einer beginnen. => Instead of everyone talking, one person should begin.|The alternative clause is not the purpose of the main action; it is the option being replaced.
152|exception: außer dass … / außer … zu|Außer dass adds an exceptional fact to an otherwise limited statement. It has its own subject and final finite verb. Außer with a zu-infinitive names an exceptional action where its actor is understood.~These expressions often follow negatives such as nichts or a statement that everything else is settled. Distinguish absence with ohne, an alternative with statt and a purpose with um. Außer plus a noun phrase is a separate prepositional frame rather than a finite clause.|Ich weiß nichts, außer dass sie hier wohnt. => I know nothing except that she lives here.~Er tat nichts, außer eine Nachricht zu schreiben. => He did nothing except write a message.|The exceptional action or fact is included, unlike an accompanying action negated by ohne.
153|sofern: provided that; soweit/soviel ich weiß: knowledge limit|Sofern sets a proviso on the main claim: it applies only if the subordinate condition holds. The clause's finite verb goes last.~Soweit ich weiß and soviel ich weiß limit the statement to the speaker's knowledge rather than guarantee it. Soweit can also restrict extent or feasibility: soweit es möglich ist. Use the actual phrase and context; a literal spatial range such as soweit das Auge reicht has another interpretation.|Das Angebot gilt, sofern noch Plätze frei sind. => The offer applies provided places are still available.~Soweit ich weiß, beginnt der Kurs morgen. => As far as I know, the course starts tomorrow.|Do not treat a knowledge disclaimer as a promise that the main claim is certainly true.
154|finite verb first, … consequence = condition without wenn|A condition can omit wenn and start with its finite verb: Regnet es, bleiben wir hier. The following main clause supplies the consequence.~Sollte/ sollten often introduces a tentative contingency in notices: Sollte der Kurs ausfallen, … Unlike a genuine yes/no question, this verb-first clause belongs to the larger conditional sentence. Use the comma and context to recognize its role. The target is understanding the condition, not treating every verb-first clause as conditional.|Brauchen Sie Hilfe, rufen Sie uns an. => If you need help, call us.~Sollte der Termin ausfallen, erhalten Sie Bescheid. => If the appointment should be canceled, you will be notified.|A separate question with a question mark and no consequence may be a real request for information instead.
155|wegen/trotz/infolge + genitive; aus/vor + dative|These noun phrases compress relations otherwise expressed by clauses. Wegen names a cause, trotz an obstacle despite which something happens, and infolge a resulting causal circumstance.~Formal written wegen, trotz and infolge commonly use genitive. Aus plus a feeling can state an intentional motive, while vor plus a feeling can explain an involuntary reaction, as in vor Angst zittern. Each preposition also has other uses, so interpret its noun and event together rather than assigning one meaning universally.|Trotz des Regens fand das Fest statt. => The celebration took place despite the rain.~Vor Aufregung konnte er nicht schlafen. => He could not sleep because of excitement.|Trotz does not mean the rain caused the event; it presents the rain as an obstacle that did not prevent it.
156|wo: location; wohin: destination; woher: source|A spatial clause can identify a place, destination or origin. Use wo for where a situation is located, wohin for where movement leads and woher for where it comes from.~These W-words introduce verb-final subordinate clauses after expressions such as dort or after an appropriate questioning/reporting frame. Keep location, destination and origin distinct even if casual speech sometimes uses wo more broadly. The clause's spatial role is separate from the main clause's word order.|Wir bleiben dort, wo die Brücke steht. => We stay where the bridge stands.~Sie zeigt mir, wohin ich gehen soll. => She shows me where I should go.|Woher asks about the source of movement or information, not its destination.
157|whole preceding fact → was / worüber / womit …|A following relative-like clause can comment on an entire preceding proposition: Er kam zu spät, was uns ärgerte. It does not necessarily describe a single preceding noun.~A wo-compound keeps the preposition required inside the comment: worüber wir uns freuten, womit niemand gerechnet hatte. A gendered noun relative instead follows an antecedent's gender/number and its own case. Also distinguish an embedded W-question and a spatial wo-clause; not every W-clause refers to a whole proposition.|Sie kam rechtzeitig, worüber wir uns freuten. => She arrived on time, which pleased us.~Er sagte ab, womit niemand gerechnet hatte. => He canceled, which nobody had expected.|Ask what the comment takes up: the whole fact of arriving/canceling or one particular noun.
158|wer/wen/wem …; alles/nichts/etwas, was …|A free relative needs no specific preceding noun: Wer helfen möchte … means anyone who wants to help. Choose wer, wen or wem from the verb's requirement inside that clause.~A resumptive pronoun in the main clause has its own independently required case: Wer hilft, dem danken wir. It may be unnecessary in a simpler construction. Was is commonly used after alles, nichts and etwas and can also introduce a free relative about content.|Wer teilnehmen möchte, muss sich anmelden. => Anyone wishing to participate must register.~Das ist alles, was ich weiß. => That is everything I know.|Do not force the main clause's case onto the free relative; wer and dem can correctly refer to the same person in different roles.
159|noun/name, explanatory noun phrase, …|An apposition identifies or explains the same person or thing as another noun phrase. A loose explanatory apposition is normally set off by commas; if inserted, it needs both boundaries.~Where case is visibly marked, the apposition usually matches the surrounding noun phrase's case. Names, titles and date expressions have additional established patterns, so prioritize recognizing their shared reference over inventing a universal ending rule. An apposition is a noun phrase, not a separate finite relative clause.|Anna, meine Schwester, kommt morgen. => Anna, my sister, is coming tomorrow.~Wir sprechen mit Herrn Bauer, unserem Nachbarn. => We are speaking with Mr Bauer, our neighbor.|Both expressions refer to the same participant; the comma does not introduce a new action by another person.
160|dative experiencer; impersonal passive; subjectless instruction|In mir ist kalt, the dative pronoun names the person experiencing a state rather than a nominative actor. Subjectless infinitive notices such as Nicht öffnen! address the reader without naming a subject.~An impersonal passive such as Hier wird getanzt describes an activity without a named agent. Introductory Es wird hier getanzt can lose es when another constituent comes first. Weather es in Es regnet is a different formal subject, not a concrete person. Identify the construction before searching for an actor.|Mir ist kalt. => I feel cold.~Hier wird nicht geraucht. => Smoking is not permitted here.|A German sentence need not contain an explicit nominative person just because an English translation uses one.
161|kosten + accusative person + accusative amount/resource|Kosten can take the affected person and an amount, time or resource in accusative: es kostet mich einen Tag. The person is not a dative recipient in this frame.~Some other constructions also contain two accusative complements, such as jemanden einen Experten nennen, although their detailed grammatical roles differ. Recognize the particular verb frame instead of transferring the recipient-plus-thing pattern from geben. Pronouns make the difference visible: mich/dich/ihn rather than mir/dir/ihm.|Die Reparatur kostet mich einen Arbeitstag. => The repair costs me a working day.~Sie nennt ihn einen guten Freund. => She calls him a good friend.|A human complement is not automatically dative; learn the case governed by this verb and meaning.
162|es … later dass-/infinitive clause|Anticipatory es is a placeholder for a later content or infinitive clause. It can anticipate a subject clause, as in Es freut mich, dass …, or act as an object correlate, as in Ich finde es wichtig, dass …~It does not refer to a concrete previously named neuter noun. Distinguish weather es and first-position es in an impersonal passive. Whether the correlate is required or optional depends on the governing construction; do not delete every es mechanically when a later clause appears.|Ich finde es wichtig, dass alle Bescheid wissen. => I think it important that everyone is informed.~Es freut mich, dich wiederzusehen. => I am pleased to see you again.|To interpret anticipatory es, look forward to the content it announces rather than only backward for a noun.
163|evaluation/feeling + wenn + recurring situation|After expressions such as ich mag es, es freut mich or es ärgert mich, a wenn-clause can name the recurring situation being liked or evaluated.~This is more than simply an unrelated condition for a practical consequence: the helping or lateness itself is what pleases or annoys. The surface clause still uses final verb order and a comma. Context can overlap with temporal/conditional meanings, so identify the evaluation's content rather than classifying every wenn mechanically as only one relation.|Es freut mich, wenn alle mithelfen. => It pleases me when everyone helps.~Ich mag es, wenn wir zusammen lernen. => I like it when we study together.|Wann asks for an event's time; it does not replace evaluative wenn in these examples.
164|detached topic + resumptive pronoun; clause + afterthought|In spoken left dislocation, a topic is placed before the clause and resumed by a pronoun: Den Kurs, den finde ich gut. The pronoun supplies its grammatical role inside the main clause.~An afterthought adds identifying material after an already complete clause: Er ist da, unser Nachbar. Neither structure should automatically be read as a relative clause. These patterns help organize spoken information; a neutral integrated sentence is usually simpler for formal production. Recognition is the goal here.|Das Buch, das lese ich gerade. => That book, I am reading it at the moment.~Sie ist angekommen, meine Schwester. => She has arrived, my sister.|In Den Kurs, den finde ich gut, finde is a main-clause verb; it is not the final verb of a relative clause.
165|es ist/sind + focused element + relative clause|A cleft highlights one element of a proposition by splitting it into an identifying frame and a relative clause. The focused person or thing supplies the point of emphasis.~Introductory es is not a concrete object in the event. The relative pronoun's case depends on its role inside the relative clause: Es ist Paul, den ich angerufen habe. Interpret the shared proposition and the focus rather than treating the two parts as unrelated facts. This is mainly a recognition target.|Es ist die Unterschrift, die fehlt. => It is the signature that is missing.~Es ist Anna, die den Kurs organisiert. => It is Anna who organizes the course.|A cleft's emphasis does not itself negate every other person or thing; use context to infer any contrast.
166|early W-element → semantic role in a later event|Interleaved reporting can separate a question element from the verb that gives it its semantic role. Find the event being asked about, its subject and the missing object; do not attach every early element to the nearest reporting verb.~Inserted comments such as glaubst du can interrupt an otherwise ordinary question. Some extraction patterns with dass are marked or regional and receive differing judgements. Interpret such material when encountered, but use a transparent neutral alternative in your own formal writing.|Wen hat sie, glaubst du, eingeladen? => Who do you think she invited?~Wen glaubst du, dass sie eingeladen hat? (markiert) => Who do you think she invited? (marked construction)|A clearer production model is Wen hat sie deiner Meinung nach eingeladen? The target here is understanding dependencies, not copying marked syntax.
167|question form + context/intonation → interactional function|A question can criticize, express astonishment or challenge a previous demand rather than request missing information. A rhetorical question's intended point is recoverable from the situation.~A counterquestion responds by questioning an assumption: Warum denn ich? Ordinary questions still seek real answers. The same words can have different functions with different intonation or context, so a question mark or verb-first form alone is not enough. Recognize the speaker's communicative purpose before translating literally.|Lärm nachts: Muss das wirklich sein? => Noise at night: Is that really necessary? (criticism)~Ein unerwarteter Erfolg: Wer hätte das gedacht! => An unexpected success: Who would have thought it! (surprise)|Do not assume every question expects a factual answer; use the explicit conversational context.
168|regional standard variation: auxiliary, gender and lexical frame|With literal sitting, standing or lying, haben is widely used, while sein also occurs in southern German, Austrian and Swiss standard varieties. The variation does not make every auxiliary choice acceptable for every verb or idiom.~Noun gender can vary too: der Joghurt and, especially in Austria/Switzerland, das Joghurt are recognized standard forms. Other regional government and verb-form differences must be learned individually. Understand established variants, while keeping your own production variety consistent and distinguishing them from unrelated case/person errors.|Ich bin auf der Bank gesessen. (regionaler Standard) => I sat on the bench. (regional standard)~Das Joghurt ist kalt. (besonders österreichisch/schweizerisch) => The yogurt is cold. (especially Austrian/Swiss usage)|Regional variation is specific, not a license to mix arbitrary cases, noun genders or conjugation endings.
169|comparative + als-clause; equality + wie-clause|A comparison can contain a full finite clause rather than just a noun phrase. Use als after a comparative and wie after so, genauso or ebenso for equality.~The finite comparative clause takes a comma and final verb order. Its tense may refer to an earlier expectation or belief: als ich gedacht hatte. A simple phrase comparison such as größer als ich normally has no clause boundary to mark. Distinguish a comparison with an expectation from a consequence with so … dass.|Der Kurs war leichter, als ich erwartet hatte. => The course was easier than I had expected.~Alles lief so, wie wir es geplant hatten. => Everything went as we had planned it.|A complete finite clause still obeys subordinate order even when its introductory word looks like an ordinary phrase-level comparison.
170|verb-first exclamation; wie + emphatic clause|German can express surprise, admiration or disapproval with verb-first exclamations such as Ist das schön! Wie-led exclamations often have final verb order: Wie gut du sprichst!~Intonation, punctuation and context distinguish these from genuine yes/no or W-questions. A question-shaped exclamation does not necessarily seek an answer; it emphasizes an unexpected fact or degree. Interpret the emotional effect rather than assuming that every inverted sentence is a request for information. Recognition of these patterns is the priority.|Ist das eine Überraschung! => What a surprise!~Wie schnell die Zeit vergangen ist! => How quickly time has passed!|Compare a genuine Wie spät ist es? with an emphatic Wie spät es schon ist! Their functions and word order differ.
